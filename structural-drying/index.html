What structural drying is, and why windows are not enough

Structural drying and dehumidification in Laguna Hills is the process of removing water that soaked into a building after the standing water is gone. Drywall, framing, subfloor and concrete hold moisture inside, and it leaves only as vapor.

Opening windows lets some of that vapor out, but it cannot control the air. A dehumidifier pulls vapor out of the air and keeps the room at conditions where materials keep releasing water. That is why equipment, not fresh air, finishes the job.

This page covers the drying process in depth. For the first 72 hours after a loss, see water mitigation.

How water moves from liquid to vapor

Wet materials dry from the surface first. Water deeper inside moves outward slowly, then evaporates into the air around it. Air movers speed the surface step by moving air across it, and dehumidifiers keep that air dry enough to take on more vapor.

Southern California air is not always wet, and the National Weather Service lists a water-year normal of 11.18 inches at John Wayne Airport, a nearby station. Even so, winter storm weeks can raise indoor humidity, and a closed-up house with wet drywall can hold much more moisture than outdoor air does. The dehumidifier does the work either way.

Finding the water: mapping and imaging

Drying starts with knowing exactly what is wet.

Moisture meters

Pin and pinless meters read drywall, wood and subfloor. The crew records each reading on a sketch of the room.

Thermal imaging camera

Wet areas often look cooler on a thermal image. It shows where to measure, and meters confirm it.

Unaffected reference

The crew reads a dry piece of the same material in an unaffected room. Drying goals compare against it.

Hygrometer readings

A thermo-hygrometer logs air temperature and humidity so the crew can see whether the dehumidifiers are keeping up.

The equipment, and where it points

LGR units suit most homes because they work well in warm rooms. Desiccant units are for cooler spaces or very wet materials.

Containing the wet zone

Drying works faster when the wet area is its own small room. The crew closes doors, covers openings with plastic sheeting and keeps outside air from refilling the space with moisture.

Safety first: before the equipment arrives

  • Turn off the water source at the main valve if the leak is not already stopped
  • Cut power only at a dry panel, and call 911 or the utility if the panel area is wet
  • Keep people and pets out of the wet zone until the crew has checked it
  • Photograph the damage and any water lines on walls before things are moved

Daily visits and drying goals

A drying job is checked, not just started.

  1. Set up and record

    The crew places air movers and dehumidifiers, then writes down starting moisture readings and room conditions.

  2. Return each day

    A technician rereads the same spots, adjusts equipment and moves air movers as wet areas shrink.

  3. Compare to the reference

    The goal is for readings to match the dry reference material, not a number picked in advance.

  4. Stop when the goal is met

    Once wet spots read like the dry reference, the equipment is removed and the readings are kept as a record.

How long equipment stays

Typical drying for a minor leak runs 1 to 3 days, and larger losses take longer. That is an industry rule of thumb, not a standard, and the readings decide, not a calendar.

Thick materials, closed wall cavities, concrete and cold weather all slow the process. If a reading stalls, the crew may open a wall, add equipment or change the setup instead of leaving it running.

Noise, heat and power

Expect a steady hum from air movers and dehumidifiers, and the room will feel warm because dehumidifiers give off heat. A few units on separate circuits is normal, so ask the crew about the load before you run the oven or a space heater.

Why speed and standards matter

The EPA says to dry water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours and that the key to mold control is moisture control. The CDPH guide Mold in My Home adds that fans should not be run if more than 48 hours have passed since flooding, because spores can spread, and that spore testing is not a first step. If your loss is older than that, tell dispatch so the crew can plan.

ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard for professional water damage restoration. Ask any company whether its crews work to it, and ask how it will document dry readings.
